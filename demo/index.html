TimeNet Hackathon · Round 1 · offshore oil wells

Can a language model read a well it has never seen?

When an offshore well develops a fault, such as a safety valve closing by itself, hydrate blocking a line or unstable flow, production stops and safety and spill risks rise. We brought Petrobras' 3W dataset into TimeNet, fine-tuned OpenTSLM to name the event from six well sensors, and scored it only on wells it never saw in training.

Why we split by well

A random split makes the same model look 2.6× better

Windows of one recording overlap, and windows of one well share its sensors and operating point. Split at random, a model meets the test well in training. Only a split by well tells you how it does on a new well, so every number on this page uses one.

Signal test · 6 perturbations of every window

Does the model read the signal, or the numbers in its prompt?

OpenTSLM gets each series twice: the normalized curve goes to its time-series encoder, and the mean and standard deviation are written into the text prompt. Each test breaks one of the two channels and leaves the other intact.

Known limits

Few wells per event

In the test split, three event types come from a single well each, so a per-class score rests on 2 to 8 recordings of one well.

A tie, not a win

OpenTSLM and the baseline are statistically tied on macro-F1. OpenTSLM raises far more false alarms; it does better on the two hydrate events and misses flow instability.

Validation did not transfer

OpenTSLM scored 0.51 on the validation wells and 0.35 on test. Validation flow instability came from a single well.

Explanations

A version trained to describe what the sensors did, with every claim checked against the data, gave no robust gain, so the model shown here answers with the event type only.
